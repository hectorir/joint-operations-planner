# JP 5-0 Joint Planning — Practitioner's Field Guide

A working planner's distillation of **Joint Publication 5-0, *Joint Planning*** (Joint Chiefs of Staff, 1 December 2020). Built for use, not study: definitions are sharp, frameworks are reproducible, and the cross-references point back to the source publication so you can dig deeper without scanning the whole 358 pages.

> "Plans are worthless, but planning is everything." — Eisenhower (epigraph to JP 5-0 Chapter I)

## 1. Quick Reference Card

### The whole publication in one sentence
Joint planning is the deliberate process of determining **how (ways)** to use **military capabilities (means)** in time and space to achieve **objectives (ends)** within an acceptable level of **risk**.

### The four planning functions (sequential, but often concurrent)
1. **Strategic Guidance** — receive direction; understand the problem.
2. **Concept Development** — frame, develop COAs, get COA approval.
3. **Plan Development** — refine the chosen COA into an executable plan/order.
4. **Plan Assessment** — refine, adapt, terminate, or execute.

### The seven JPP steps
1. Planning Initiation
2. Mission Analysis
3. Course of Action (COA) Development
4. COA Analysis & Wargaming
5. COA Comparison
6. COA Approval
7. Plan or Order Development

### The seven principles of planning
Focused on the Objective · Globally Integrated and Coordinated · Resource Informed · Risk Informed · Framed within the Strategic & Operational Environment · Informs Decision Making · Adaptive and Flexible.

### The three operative questions of operational design
1. **Where are we?** (current OE)
2. **Where do we want to go?** (objectives, desired conditions, end state)
3. **What's the problem?** (the gap between the two — root cause, not symptoms)

### The two-line test for any plan
- Does it **achieve the objective / attain the end state** within acceptable risk?
- Does it **not foreclose future options**?

## 2. Doctrinal Foundations

### 2.1 Why joint planning exists *(JP 5-0, I-1 to I-3)*

Joint planning serves two purposes:
- **Strategic level:** provides the President and SecDef with options, costs, and risks for employing the joint force in support of the *National Security Strategy*.
- **Operational level:** translates national-level guidance into specific activities to achieve strategic and operational objectives and attain the military end state.

The character of conflict is evolving (transregional, all-domain, multifunctional), but **the nature of war remains constant — the ability to impose our will on another party to achieve a national objective through the sanctioned use of force**.

### 2.2 Strategy / strategic art / operational art / operational planning

| Concept | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Strategy** | A prudent idea or set of ideas for employing the instruments of national power in a synchronized and integrated fashion to achieve theater, national, and multinational objectives. |
| **Strategic art** | The formulation, coordination, and application of ends, ways, and means to implement policy and promote national interests. |
| **Operational art** | The cognitive approach by commanders and staffs — supported by skill, knowledge, experience, creativity, and judgment — to develop strategies, campaigns, and operations to organize and employ military forces by integrating ends, ways, means, and risks. |
| **Operational planning** | Translates strategy into executable activities, operations, and campaigns within resource and policy limitations. |

### 2.3 The Eisenhower principle
Plans become outdated the moment a crisis begins; planning builds the *understanding* that enables adaptation. Doctrine therefore privileges **shared understanding, frequent dialogue, and iterative refinement** over polished products.

### 2.4 Understanding problems before solving them *(JP 5-0, I-4)*

Three rules:
1. **Distinguish symptoms from root causes.** "What problem are we really being asked to solve?" Killing or detaining insurgents seldom addresses the underlying causes of an insurgency — military action may *exacerbate* problems rather than solve them.
2. **Decide who is best suited to lead.** It may be a non-DoD lead. Pull the right interorganizational entity in *at the start*, not after the plan is built.
3. **Write a clear, concise, precise problem statement.** It is fundamentally a *contextual hypothesis*; small differences in the hypothesis drive substantial differences in the resulting plan.

### 2.5 The seven principles of planning *(JP 5-0, I-5 to I-7)*

1. **Focused on the Objective.** Plans contribute to a national objective. A directed military end state may not equal the strategic objective envisioned by policymakers — surface gaps to DoD leaders.
2. **Globally Integrated and Coordinated.** Synchronize across CCMDs, the JS, NGB, DoD agencies, interagency, and multinational partners. Military forces alone cannot achieve national objectives.
3. **Resource Informed.** Plan with currently available capabilities, not future ones. Plans assume the apportionment tables; they do not invent forces.
4. **Risk Informed.** Identify and articulate risk; plans without explicit risk discussion are not decision-making aids.
5. **Framed within the Strategic & Operational Environment.** Adversaries set conditions in their favor — *do not* assume contentious issues away.
6. **Informs Decision Making.** Identify key assumptions, resource requirements, cost-benefit trade-offs, and risks per COA so leaders can choose.
7. **Adaptive and Flexible.** Networked, collaborative dialogue across strategic, operational, and tactical levels.

### 2.6 The shared understanding imperative *(JP 5-0, I-18 to I-20)*

- **Civilian-military dialogue.** Strategy is developed at the USG department level. Joint planning supports the interaction between SecDef, CCDRs, and the CJCS so the President and SecDef can decide *when, where, and how* to employ forces.
- **Adaptive planning** is not a separate process — it is the *ability* to develop, update, or change a plan rapidly based on changes in the OE, guidance, resources, or threat.

## 3. Strategic Guidance Architecture

### 3.1 Who issues what *(JP 5-0, II-1 to II-10)*

```
PRESIDENT
├── National Security Strategy (NSS)              ← the foundation
├── Unified Command Plan (UCP)                    ← creates CCMDs, assigns missions
├── Contingency Planning Guidance (CPG)           ← Title 10 §113 statutory product
└── Executive Orders / NSC decisions

SECDEF
├── National Defense Strategy (NDS)               ← implements NSS for DoD
├── Defense Planning Guidance / programming
└── Forces for Allocation (GFMAP)

CJCS (principal military advisor)
├── National Military Strategy (NMS)
├── Joint Strategic Campaign Plan (JSCP)          ← directs CCDR campaign plans
├── Global Integration Frameworks (GIFs)          ← cross-CCMD crisis frameworks
└── Joint Strategic Planning System (JSPS)

CCDRs
└── Combatant Command Campaign Plans (CCPs)       ← daily operations, shape OE
    └── Subordinate plans: contingency, posture, country-specific security cooperation
```

### 3.2 The Joint Planning & Execution Community (JPEC) *(JP 5-0, II-10 to II-13)*

Three roles to keep straight:
- **Supported CCDR** — primary responsibility for the assigned task; runs the planning effort.
- **Supporting commanders** — provide forces/assistance per global force management policy; produce supporting plans.
- **Coordinating authority** — delegated to coordinate functions and activities involving multiple Services/components/agencies (cannot direct, only coordinate).

### 3.3 Campaign-plan taxonomy

| Type | Owner | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| **Global Campaign Plan (GCP)** | Managed by CJCS as global integrator | Threats/challenges affecting US interests across the globe; spans most/all CCMDs. |
| **Regional Campaign Plan (RCP)** | SecDef-directed coordinating CCDR | Regional issues across multiple CCMDs but below GCP threshold. |
| **Functional Campaign Plan (FCP)** | Functional CCDR | Functional, non-geographic threats across CCMDs. |
| **Combatant Command Campaign Plan (CCP)** | CCDR | Day-to-day activities within their AOR/functional area; the **centerpiece** of CCMD planning. |

### 3.4 Contingency vs. crisis planning *(figure I-1)*

| | **Contingency** | **Crisis** |
|---|---|---|
| Time | 6+ months | Hours to ~12 months |
| Assumptions | Heavy use | Minimal — rely on facts |
| Output | Plan | Order / execution |
| Key task | SecDef approves planning | President/SecDef approve COA |
| Final approval | SecDef | President / SecDef |

## 4. Planning Products & Orders

### 4.1 The four levels of planning detail *(JP 5-0, I-11 to I-12)*

| Level | Product | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Commander's Estimate | Multiple COAs, recommends one. Lowest detail. |
| **2** | Base Plan (BPLAN) | CONOPS, major forces, support concepts, timelines. No annexes. |
| **3** | Concept Plan (CONPLAN) | OPLAN in abbreviated form. Includes annexes A, B, C, D, J, K, S, V, Z. Optional TPFDD (level 3T). |
| **4** | Operation Plan (OPLAN) | Complete plan with all annexes, full TPFDD, and TPFDL. Used when contingency threatens national security and detail is required. |

### 4.2 Joint orders cheat-sheet *(figure I-2)*

| Order | Purpose | SecDef approval? |
|---|---|---|
| **WARNORD** | Initiate COA development; request commander's estimate | Only if includes deployment actions |
| **PLANORD** | Begin planning for an anticipated COA | No |
| **ALERTORD** | Begin execution planning on a SecDef-selected COA (does **not** authorize execution) | Yes |
| **OPORD** | Direct coordinated execution | Operation-specific |
| **PTDO** (Prepare to Deploy) | Increase/decrease deployability posture | Yes if force allocation |
| **DEPORD** | Allocate/transfer forces; deploy/redeploy | Yes if force movement |
| **EXORD** | Implement an approved CONOPS — *only the President or SecDef approve* | Yes |
| **FRAGORD** | Modify a previously issued OPORD | No |

### 4.3 Five-paragraph order format
Per CJCSM 3130.03, the standard format is **Situation, Mission, Execution, Sustainment, Command & Signal** with annexes (A Task Org, B Intel, C Ops, D Logistics, J C2, K C5, S Special Tech, V Interagency, Z Distribution at minimum).

## 5. The Joint Planning Process (JPP) — Seven Steps

> The JPP is a problem-solving technique. It is not prescriptive — modify, truncate, or run steps concurrently as the situation demands. Steps **4-7 are repeated** as new requirements emerge during plan development.

### 5.1 Visualization

```
Step 1: Planning Initiation
   ↓
Step 2: Mission Analysis  ←──── re-enter as facts/assumptions change
   ↓
Step 3: COA Development
   ↓
Step 4: COA Analysis & Wargaming  ─┐
   ↓                                │ iterate
Step 5: COA Comparison             │
   ↓                                │
Step 6: COA Approval ──────────────┘
   ↓
Step 7: Plan or Order Development → Transition → Execution
```

### 5.2 Step 1 — Planning Initiation *(JP 5-0, III-11)*

- Triggered by recognition that military capability may be needed (presidential directive, NSS, UCP, CPG, NDS, NMS, JSCP) or by a CCDR identifying a planning requirement.
- The commander provides **initial planning guidance** based on current understanding of the OE, problem, and operational approach.
- Operational design supports this step by building understanding of strategic direction, the strategic environment, and the OE — which lets the commander **define the problem** before mission analysis begins.

### 5.3 Step 2 — Mission Analysis *(JP 5-0, III-12 to III-32)*

The most important step. Mission analysis lays the foundation for everything that follows.

**Key Inputs**
Higher HQ planning directive · Strategic direction · Commander's planning guidance (OE description, problem definition, operational approach, initial intent) · Strategic estimate · JIPOE · Network analysis.

**Key Outputs**
- Mission statement (who/what/when/where/why)
- Commander's refined operational approach (intent statement, updated planning guidance)
- Initial CCIRs, friendly + threat COGs, identified essential/specified/implied tasks
- Mission analysis briefing, planning directive, COA evaluation criteria
- Initial risk assessment

**The 14 mission analysis activities** *(figure III-6 — not strictly sequential)*
1. Begin logistics supportability analysis.
2. Analyze higher HQ planning activities and strategic guidance.
3. Review commander's initial planning guidance.
4. Determine known facts and develop planning assumptions.
5. Determine and analyze operational limitations.
6. Determine specified, implied, and essential tasks.
7. Develop mission statement.
8. Conduct initial force identification.
9. Develop risk assessment.
10. Develop COA evaluation criteria.
11. Develop initial military objectives.
12. Develop CCIRs.
13. Prepare staff estimates.
14. Prepare and deliver mission analysis brief.
15. Publish updated planning guidance, intent statement, and refined operational approach.

#### 5.3.1 Facts vs. assumptions

| | **Fact** | **Assumption** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Information known to be true | Supposition presumed true based on available facts |
| Test | Verifiable | Logical, realistic, **essential for planning to continue** |
| Discipline | Acknowledge HQ assumptions; raise if you have info that invalidates them | Keep to the *minimum required*; each adds error and bias |
| Required action | None | Identify CCIR to validate or invalidate; if invalidated, alter plan |

> **Discipline:** *"Commanders and staffs should never assume away adversary capabilities or assume unrealistic friendly capabilities will be available."*

#### 5.3.2 Constraints vs. restraints

| | **Constraint** | **Restraint** |
|---|---|---|
| Posture | "Must do" | "Cannot do" |
| Example | Eisenhower required to enter Europe rather than rely on strategic bombing | MacArthur prohibited from striking Chinese targets north of the Yalu |

#### 5.3.3 Specified · implied · essential tasks

- **Specified** — assigned in the planning directive ("Defend Country Green from attack by Country Red").
- **Implied** — necessary to accomplish specified tasks but not assigned ("Establish maritime superiority out to 50 miles…", "BPT FID/SFA"). Excludes routine tasks (recon, flank protection).
- **Essential** — must succeed to attain the directed end state. Determined from the lists of specified and implied.

#### 5.3.4 Mission statement template
> When directed [**when**], United States X Command, in concert with coalition partners [**who**], deters Country Y from coercing its neighbors and proliferating weapons of mass destruction [**what**] to maintain security [**why**] in the region [**where**].

#### 5.3.5 CCIRs — Commander's Critical Information Requirements *(figure III-11)*

> CCIRs belong **exclusively to the commander**. Situation-dependent, focused on predictable events/activities, time-sensitive, established by order or plan.

| CCIR family | Focus | Drives |
|---|---|---|
| **PIR** (Priority Intelligence Requirement) | Adversary and OE | Intelligence collection — links to NAIs, decision points |
| **FFIR** (Friendly Force Information Requirement) | Friendly capabilities, status | Operational reporting aligned to joint functions |

Keep the list short. Update it throughout planning, assessment, and execution.

#### 5.3.6 Risk assessment scaffolding *(figures III-8 to III-10)*

| Probability | Range | | Consequence | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Very Likely | ~81–100% | | Extreme | major harm to something of value |
| Probable | ~51–80% | | Major | |
| Improbable | ~21–50% | | Moderate | |
| Highly Unlikely | ~0–20% | | Minor | |

Risk levels (resulting from probability × consequence): **High** (critical objectives unachievable) · **Significant** (only most critical achievable) · **Moderate** (partial) · **Low** (full attainment likely).

Two flavors:
- **Operational risk / risk-to-mission** — probability and consequence of failing to achieve current/future military objectives.
- **Force management risk / risk-to-force** — probability and consequence of breaking the force ("breaking the force") through over-rotation.

### 5.4 Step 3 — COA Development *(JP 5-0, III-33 to III-43)*

Each COA must answer **who, what, where, when, why, and how**.

**Required components of every COA** *(elaborated)*
- Initial COA sketches and statements
- Component-level missions/tasks (in joint-function terms; with graphic control measures)
- Task organization and command relationships
- Sustainment concept
- Deployment concept
- Sequencing (simultaneous/sequential/combination)
- Main effort and supporting efforts by phase
- Decision points and assessment process
- COG and decisive points focus
- Definition of the OA (AOR, theater of war, JOA, JSOA, AOA, etc.)
- Nuclear planning (USSTRATCOM coordination if relevant)
- SAP integration as appropriate

#### 5.4.1 The five COA validity tests *(JP 5-0, III-41 to III-42)*
A COA is **only valid** if it passes **all five**:

1. **Suitable** — accomplishes the mission within commander's guidance.
2. **Feasible** — accomplishable within established time, space, and resource limitations.
3. **Acceptable** — balances cost and risk against advantage gained; no unacceptable risk; reconciled with US/USG law, ROE, policy.
4. **Distinguishable** — sufficiently different from other COAs (focus/main effort, scheme of maneuver, sequencing, primary mechanism, task org, use of reserves).
5. **Complete** — answers who/what/where/when/how/why; covers objectives, effects, tasks, forces, deployment/employment/sustainment, time estimates, and end state criteria.

> "The staff should reject COA alternatives that do not meet all five validity criteria."

### 5.5 Step 4 — COA Analysis & Wargaming *(JP 5-0, III-44 to III-55)*

Wargaming is the **primary means** for COA analysis. Each retained COA is wargamed against the most likely *and* the most dangerous enemy COAs. **Major revisions are not made mid-wargame** — stop, revise, restart.

**Cell structure**
- **Red cell** — J-2-led; aggressively plays the enemy with full realistic capabilities.
- **Blue cell** — friendly force.
- **Green cell** — transnational groups, NGOs, and other relevant actors.
- **White cell** — senior arbitrators; rules of the wargame; adjudication.

**Method: action → reaction → counteraction**, iterated through critical events. Three manual approaches:
- Deliberate timeline analysis (most thorough — day-by-day)
- Phasing
- Critical events / sequence of essential tasks

**Outputs**
- Wargamed COAs with branches and sequels identified
- Synchronization matrix
- Decision support template (DST) and decision support matrix (DSM)
- Refined CCIRs and event template/matrix
- Newly identified resource shortfalls
- Refined staff estimates
- Assessment plan and criteria

### 5.6 Step 5 — COA Comparison *(JP 5-0, III-55 to III-58)*

- COAs are evaluated **independently against each evaluation criterion**, not against each other.
- Comparison facilitates the commander's decision by balancing the **ends, means, ways, and risk** of each COA.
- Methods: numerical matrix (with weights), descriptive matrix, plus/minus/neutral matrix (Appendix F).

**Three questions to answer:**
1. What are the differences between each COA?
2. What are the advantages and disadvantages?
3. What are the risks?

### 5.7 Step 6 — COA Approval *(JP 5-0, III-59 to III-62)*

The commander's options are not limited to the staff recommendation. They can:
1. Concur as presented.
2. Concur with modifications.
3. Select a different COA.
4. Combine COAs to create a new COA.
5. Reject all and restart at COA development or mission analysis.
6. Defer the decision.

After selection, the staff produces a **clear, concise decision statement** in simple language, including a statement of **acceptable risk**.

### 5.8 Step 7 — Plan or Order Development *(JP 5-0, III-62 to III-74)*

The CONOPS becomes the centerpiece of the plan/order. Plan development activities:

- **Force planning** — identify all forces required (CONOPS-driven; phased; mission-priority sequenced); develop TPFDD.
- **Support planning** — concept of logistics support, logistics supportability analysis (LSA), transportation refinement.
- **Deployment & redeployment planning** — JRSOI, contested-environment considerations, force rotations.
- **Shortfall identification** — limiting factors; risks. Plan development *does not halt* awaiting shortfall resolution.
- **Feasibility analysis** — forces, sustainment, transportation.
- **Documentation** — TPFDD complete, transportation feasible.
- **Plan review and approval** — JS J-5 coordinates JPEC review; CJCS recommends; SecDef/President approves.
- **Supporting plan development** — subordinate/supporting commands write plans nested under the supported plan.

#### 5.8.1 Main effort & supporting efforts
- Designate as soon as possible.
- Can be defined **geographically** or **functionally**.
- Main effort can shift across phases — when it shifts, **support priorities must shift with it**.
- Secondary efforts complement or enable the main effort; they must not divert resources from it.

#### 5.8.2 Transition (within plan development)
- **Transition Brief** to subordinate/supporting/adjacent commanders covers: higher mission/intent, mission, intent, CCIRs, task org, situation, neutral networks, CONOPS, execution (branches/sequels), planning support tools.
- **Confirmation Brief** — subordinate-led, back to the higher commander, to confirm understanding of intent and tasks and surface gaps.
- **Transition Drills** — sand tables, map exercises, rehearsals.

## 6. Operational Design Methodology

> Operational design is the **analytical framework that underpins planning** — used to make sense of complex problems before (and alongside) the procedural rigor of the JPP.

### 6.1 The nine-step methodology *(JP 5-0, IV-2 to IV-3)*

1. Understand the strategic direction and guidance.
2. Understand the strategic environment (policies, diplomacy, politics).
3. Understand the OE and relevant contested environments.
4. **Define the problem** (create shared understanding; plan with uncertainty).
5. Identify assumptions (strategic and operational).
6. **Develop options** — the operational approach.
7. Identify decisions and decision points (external to the organization).
8. Refine the operational approach.
9. Develop planning and assessment guidance.

### 6.2 Frameworks for understanding the OE

**Holistic view dimensions** *(figure IV-2)* — Friendly · Neutral · Enemy/Adversary · Other Actors, viewed across:
- **Physical** (land, air, maritime, surface/subsurface, space, cyberspace, EMS)
- **Informational** (cognitive + informational + physical dimensions)
- **Time and sociocultural factors**

**PMESII** — Political, Military, Economic, Social, Information, Infrastructure.
**ASCOPE** — Areas, Structures, Capabilities, Organizations, People, Events.
**METT-T** — Mission, Enemy, Terrain & weather, Troops & support, Time available.

### 6.3 Defining the problem *(figure IV-3)*

A planner asks:
- Which issues are critical (to US, to allies, to host nation)?
- Are resources sufficient to address them all?
- What's the relationship between issues — which fester if ignored, which resolve themselves, which are "pet rocks" and not really issues?
- Will a partial solution cause more problems?

The problem statement identifies:
1. Tensions between current and desired conditions.
2. OE elements that must change (or remain unchanged) — including relevant actor behaviors.
3. Opportunities and threats.
4. Operational limitations.

### 6.4 Operational approach *(figure IV-5)*

The **operational approach** is the commander's description of the broad actions the force will take to achieve the objective or attain the military end state. It is the **bridge from the problem to the JPP** and provides:
- The commander's visualization of how operations transform current conditions into desired conditions.
- The model for execution and assessment design.
- A better shared understanding of the OE and the problem.

Once the SecDef approves the approach, it is the basis for beginning, continuing, or completing detailed planning.

## 7. Elements of Operational Design

> Twelve+ elements grouped in four categories: **overarching**, **space (OE)**, **time**, **forces**. Not all elements are required for all plans — they are tools.

### 7.1 The categories *(JP 5-0, IV-18 to IV-20)*

| Category | Elements |
|---|---|
| **Overarching** | Objective, Military End State, COG, Effects, Culmination |
| **Space (OE)** | Lines of Operation (LOOs), Lines of Effort (LOEs), Decisive Points, Direct/Indirect Approach, Operational Reach |
| **Time** | Arranging Operations (simultaneity, depth, timing, tempo, phasing), Anticipation, Operational Pause, Branches & Sequels |
| **Forces** | Forces and Functions, Force Employment Mechanisms (Defeat/Stabilization/Competition) |

### 7.2 Objective vs. End State vs. Effects vs. Tasks *(figure IV-9)*

```
National strategic   →   Strategic end state
                              └─ Objectives

Theater strategic    →   Military end state
                              └─ Objectives → Effects → Tasks

Operational          →   Military end state
                              └─ Objectives → Effects → Tasks

Tactical             →   Mission
                              └─ Objectives → Tasks
```

- **End state** = set of conditions to meet conflict termination criteria.
- **Objective** = clearly defined, decisive, attainable goal. Specific, unambiguous, **does not imply ways/means**, **not written as a task**. Single result.
- **Effect** = a physical/behavioral state of a system that results from an action. *Desired effect* supports an objective; *undesired effect* inhibits it. Effect statements should be measurable, link directly to objectives, not specify ways/means, and be distinguishable from the objective they support.
- **Task** = friendly action to create a desired effect or preclude an undesired effect.

> **The objective is the single most important element of operational design and should be determined first.**

### 7.3 Center of Gravity *(deeper treatment in §8)*

> **The COG is the source of power or strength that enables a military force to achieve its objective and is what an opposing force can orient its actions against that will lead to enemy failure.**

### 7.4 Culmination *(JP 5-0, IV-28)*

The point in time/space when the operation can no longer maintain momentum.
- **Offensive:** when continuing the attack is no longer possible — must shift to defense or pause.
- **Defensive:** when the defender can no longer counterattack or hold.
- **Stabilization:** erosion of national will, declining popular support, legitimacy questions, political resolution.

The JFC's job: **secure the objective before culmination**, and keep sustainment synchronized to forestall it.

### 7.5 LOOs vs. LOEs *(JP 5-0, IV-29 to IV-32)*

| | **Line of Operation (LOO)** | **Line of Effort (LOE)** |
|---|---|---|
| Logic | Time/space/positional | Purpose — cause and effect |
| Use | Combat operations with positional reference to an adversary | When position relative to adversary has little relevance (counterinsurgency, stability, multinational ops where unity of command is elusive) |
| Example node sequence | "Establish bases → Secure entry points → Secure ports → Seize key terrain → Secure routes → Secure objective" | Civil control · Civil security · Essential services · Economic/infrastructure development · Governance |

**Combine** LOOs and LOEs to connect objectives to a unifying purpose and incorporate non-military contributions.

**Interior vs. exterior lines:** interior = diverge from a central point (mass faster than enemy can reposition); exterior = converge on the enemy (offers encirclement/annihilation but requires stronger or more mobile force).

### 7.6 Decisive points

> **A decisive point is key terrain, key event, critical factor, or function that, when acted upon, enables a commander to gain a marked advantage over an enemy or contributes materially to achieving success.**

(Note: the 2020 revision changed the definition from "geographic place" to "key terrain" specifically to account for cyberspace operations.)

Decisive points are usually **not** COGs — they are the **keys to attacking or protecting** them. The most important decisive points emerge from critical-factors analysis.

### 7.7 Direct vs. indirect approach *(figure IV-12)*

| | **Direct** | **Indirect** |
|---|---|---|
| Target | The COG itself | Critical vulnerabilities feeding the COG |
| Precondition | Superior forces, qualitative leadership advantage, technological superiority | Cumulative effects against multiple critical vulnerabilities |
| When it dominates | Position of strength | Position of disadvantage; well-protected COG; risk-aversion to direct action; irregular warfare |

At the strategic level, indirect can include depriving the enemy of allies, sanctions, weakening national will, breaking up alliance cohesion.

### 7.8 Operational reach
The **distance and duration** across which a joint force can successfully employ military capabilities. Constrained by geography, threats, environment. Extended by forward positioning, weapon-system range, HNS/contracted support, throughput efficiency. **Inextricably tied to LOOs and basing.**

### 7.9 Arranging operations

| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Simultaneity** | Concurrent application of military and non-military power against the enemy's key capabilities and sources of strength — overloads their decision cycle. Also: concurrent ops at tactical, operational, and strategic levels. |
| **Depth** | Reach into the OE in space, time, and resources. |
| **Timing & Tempo** | Operate at a pace the enemy cannot match. Tempo can be accelerated to overwhelm or decelerated to frustrate. |
| **Phasing** | Organize a complex operation into manageable parts. Phases are **condition-driven**, not time-driven (general rule). Each phase's ending conditions = next phase's starting conditions. Phases must avoid culmination. |
| **Branches** | Planned contingencies that add flexibility — anticipate situations that could alter the basic plan. |
| **Sequels** | Subsequent operations based on possible outcomes (victory, defeat, stalemate). |
| **Operational pause** | A safety valve to forestall culmination — reconstitute, augment sustainment. Risk: forfeiting initiative; mitigate by alternating pauses among components. |

### 7.10 Anticipation *(JP 5-0, IV-39 to IV-40)*
> "Anticipation is key to effective planning. JFCs must consider what might happen and look for indicators of forecasted events. During execution, JFCs should remain alert for the unexpected and be prepared to exploit opportunities."

Risk: anticipation enables deception. Use multiple/redundant information sources.

## 8. Center of Gravity (COG) Analysis

### 8.1 Why it matters
COG identification is "**a linchpin in the planning effort**" *(JP 5-0, IV-23)*. Faulty COG analysis leads to inability to achieve strategic and operational objectives at acceptable cost.

### 8.2 Key principles
- COGs **exist in an adversarial context** — out of the relationship between adversaries.
- COGs **change over time** as the strategic environment, OE, or relative power shifts.
- COGs exist at **all levels** of warfare: at the strategic level a COG may be an alliance, political will, or national leadership; at the operational level, often a powerful military capability.
- A CCP may have **multiple COGs** if it includes operations along multiple independent LOEs.
- Without a well-defined threat (peacetime, shaping), there will often be no enemy COG.

### 8.3 The critical-factors framework *(figure IV-8)*

```
     End State / Objectives
           ▲
           │
   ┌─── Center of Gravity ───┐
   │                         │
   ├─ Critical Capabilities ─┤  Primary abilities essential to mission accomplishment
   │                         │
   ├─ Critical Requirements ─┤  Conditions/resources/means the COG needs to employ the capability
   │                         │
   └─ Critical Vulnerabilities ┘  Aspects of critical requirements vulnerable to attack
           │
           ▼
       Effects → Tasks
```

### 8.4 Identifying a COG (the analyst's playbook)
1. Identify the threat's **critical strengths**. The COG typically lives among them.
2. Decompose how the threat **organizes, fights, thinks, and decides** — physical and psychological.
3. Avoid mirror-imaging. *"A rational decision in the threat's perspective may appear irrational from the friendly perspective."*
4. Test by asking: would defeat/destruction/neutralization of this COG cause the adversary to abandon its strategic objectives or change its COA?

### 8.5 Attacking a COG: choose targets, not symbols
When selecting which critical vulnerabilities to attack, balance:
- **Criticality** to the COG vs. **accessibility** (can we reach it?)
- **Redundancy** and **resiliency** (will the adversary route around it?)
- **Impact on other military and national objectives** (collateral effects, escalation risk)

### 8.6 Five questions on completion of COG analysis
1. Will the joint force achieve its objectives if the threat COG is destroyed?
2. Does mission accomplishment lead to the objective and support the next-higher objective?
3. If destroyed, what gaps/weaknesses/vacuums emerge (2nd/3rd-order effects)?
4. Is a direct attack feasible *or* desirable? Sometimes total collapse of an enemy system creates more risk than it removes.
5. Should we strengthen or protect *our own* COG (interoperability, alliance cohesion, public will)?

> **Decisive points usually are not COGs, they are the keys to attacking or protecting them.**

## 9. Defeat, Stabilization & Competition Mechanisms

These are **force employment mechanisms** that complement COG analysis. COG analysis explains the problem; mechanisms describe the **method of solution**.

### 9.1 The three basic defeat mechanisms *(JP 5-0, IV-41)*

| Mechanism | Aim | Typical context |
|---|---|---|
| **Destruction** | Eliminate the enemy's armed forces' ability to fight as a cohesive, coordinated organization | Traditional warfare from a position of strength |
| **Attrition** | Disrupt, degrade, or neutralize war-making capability over time, with cumulative impact | Traditional warfare from a position of disadvantage — buy time and space to seize initiative |
| **Exhaustion** | Impose unacceptable costs that erode the will to continue, even if the enemy is achieving tactical/operational success | Irregular warfare — when destruction/attrition are not viable |

### 9.2 Specific defeat mechanism actions

| Action | Verb | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| **Destroy** | Eliminate forces and capabilities by combat power over time or a single decisive attack | When you can win the engagement |
| **Dislocate** | Compel enemy to expose forces by reacting to a specific action | Force the enemy into a bad choice between accepting neutralization or risking destruction |
| **Disintegrate** | Shatter enemy coherence after destruction/dislocation | Follow-on, exploit the loss of situational understanding |
| **Isolate** | Marginalize critical capabilities; limit ability to influence events | Expose adversary to continued degradation |
| **Disrupt** | Interrupt or reduce effectiveness without significantly degrading future capability | When policy/resource/risk limits prevent imposing greater cost |
| **Degrade** | Reduce ability *and/or will* to conduct future operations | When defeat is precluded by limits |
| **Deny** | Prevent achievement of strategic objectives without significantly increasing resources or accepting higher risk | Competition, irregular warfare, economy-of-force ops |
| **Neutralize** | Render ineffective without necessarily destroying | Use nonlethal weapons as intermediate force |

### 9.3 Stabilization mechanisms

| Mechanism | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Compel** | Threat or use of force to establish control, enforce cessation of hostilities, peace agreements |
| **Control** | Establish public order, secure routes/sites/populations, occupy key terrain, provide for immediate population needs |
| **Influence** | Alter opinions and attitudes — coherent narrative, civil-military teaming, nonlethal capabilities, cultural understanding |
| **Support** | Establish/reinforce/set conditions for other instruments of national power; logistical support, services, enabling capabilities for HN, USAID, DoS |

> Stabilization is **inherently political**. Misuse of force — or even the *perceived threat* of misuse — can destroy the legitimacy on which stability depends.

### 9.4 Competition mechanisms
Below the threshold of armed conflict, planners identify **competition mechanisms** — ways to maintain or establish favorable conditions when forces are employed in supporting or supported roles short of armed conflict.

## 10. Risk

### 10.1 The doctrinal definition
**Risk = probability × consequence of an event causing harm to something valued.**

Two flavors at the joint level:
- **Risk-to-mission (operational risk)** — current force's ability to achieve military objectives in the NMS at acceptable human/material/financial cost.
- **Risk-to-force (force management risk)** — Service/JFP ability to generate trained and ready forces within rotation ratios and surge capacities. The "breaking the force" risk.

### 10.2 The inverse relationship
Resources ↑ → Risk ↓. But national resources are finite — committing more to plan A creates **opportunity cost / elevated risk elsewhere**. There is no magic formula for quantification; planners give decision makers an assessment of expected risks, costs, and benefits.

### 10.3 Risk mitigation methods
1. **Reduce likelihood** — protective measures (e.g., malaria prophylaxis, infrastructure resiliency), deterrence.
2. **Reduce cost** — reactive safety measures, dispersion (so a single attack costs only capacity).
3. **Increase nonorganic support** — contracted support, host-nation support (HNS), strategic lift augmentation.
4. **Acceptance** — residual risk that cannot be eliminated; commanders must understand it before execution.

### 10.4 Communicating risk to civilians
Strategic-level risk is *primarily political* — impact on the US public, allies, adversaries, enemies, US objectives, future US status. CCDRs translate military risk into terms civilian leaders can act on:
- Quantified statements ("the mission will take 6 months versus 2 months")
- Casualty bounds ("we expect casualties to increase from x to y")
- Mission-success terms ("which missions will and will not be accomplished")

## 11. Operation Assessment

### 11.1 What it is
Operation assessment is the **continuous activity** that:
- Measures effectiveness of operations and projected effectiveness of plans against contingencies as the OE changes.
- Supports decision making by measuring progress toward task accomplishment, effect creation, objective achievement, or end-state attainment.
- Identifies when a plan needs to **refine, adapt, terminate, or execute**.

Assessment **starts with the initiation of joint planning** — not after execution begins.

### 11.2 The seven tenets of operation assessment

1. **Commander Centricity.** The commander's involvement in assessment is essential. Assessments must answer the commander's questions, not the staff's.
2. **Subordinate Commander Involvement.** Assessment supports conversations *between commanders* at different echelons.
3. **Integration.** Cross-functional staff representation is required to produce credible, rigorous assessments.
4. **Integration into the Planning Process and Battle Rhythm.** Synchronize assessment to the commander's decision cycle so information arrives in time to be useful.
5. **Integration of External Sources of Information.** Pull in external info that updates the OE picture — interagency, multinational, NGO, open source.
6. **Credibility and Transparency.** Cite all sources; show your work.
7. **Continuous.** A specific product may follow a schedule, but assessment itself never stops.

### 11.3 The assessment process *(JP 5-0 Appendix K, six steps)*

1. **Develop assessment approach** — informed by the operational design, especially end states, objectives, effects, tasks, and conditions.
2. **Develop assessment plan** — what data to collect, how, how often, who owns it.
3. **Collect information and intelligence** — aligned to the battle rhythm and decision points.
4. **Analyze information and intelligence** — link to operational design elements; surface trends and inflection points.
5. **Communicate feedback and recommendations** — what to keep, what to change, where the OE has shifted.
6. **Adapt plans for operations, campaigns, and assessment** — feed the analysis back into the JPP and the operational approach.

### 11.4 The assessment hierarchy
Tasks → Conditions → Effects → Objectives → End State
- **MOPs (Measures of Performance)** — answer "are we doing things right?" (task accomplishment).
- **MOEs (Measures of Effectiveness)** — answer "are we doing the right things?" (effect creation, objective progress).
- **Indicators** — observable, collectable items that feed MOPs/MOEs.

### 11.5 The four possible plan-assessment outcomes *(JP 5-0, III-8)*

1. **Refine** — orderly process during planning and after development; transmitted as FRAGORDs in execution rather than republishing.
2. **Adapt** — major modifications driven by changes in strategic direction, the OE, or the problem.
3. **Terminate** — recommend termination when the plan is no longer required (CPG/JSCP plans require approval to archive).
4. **Execute** — review the plan, validate assumptions, issue orders.

## 12. Transition to Execution

### 12.1 The three execution conditions *(JP 5-0 Chapter VII)*

| Condition | Trigger | Lead |
|---|---|---|
| **Contingency Plan Execution** | Anticipated crisis matches an existing plan | Plans directorate (J-5) hand-off to Operations directorate (J-3) |
| **Crisis Planning to Execution** | Emergent event — no suitable existing plan, or modify existing | Crisis planning section |
| **Campaign Plan Execution** | CCPs are in **constant** execution | The CCDR |

### 12.2 The hand-off (J-5 → J-3)

The transition brief covers:
- Higher HQ mission and intent
- Mission, commander's intent, CCIRs
- Task organization
- Friendly/enemy situation, networks, nonmilitary considerations
- CONOPS
- Execution (branches, sequels)
- Planning support tools (sync matrices, DSTs)

### 12.3 Tools to aid transition
- **Rehearsals, exercises, staff walks** — for most likely contingencies; tests transition activities and assesses staff.
- **Transition Book** — condensed primer for commander and staff; rapid overview of options and requirements for response.
- **Staff Checklists** — by functional area and timeline; actions required to support crisis execution.
- **CCIRs** — what does the commander still need to know within each staff section's responsibilities?
- **POC Lists** — critical for crisis tempo even though not formally part of the plan.

### 12.4 Plan implementation actions
Before execution, the plan must:
1. Confirm assumptions — convert to facts.
2. Model the TPFDD — confirm sourcing and transportation feasibility.
3. Establish execution timings.
4. Confirm execution authorities (President or SecDef).
5. Conduct execution sourcing — submit RFFs through the GFM process if needed.
6. Issue necessary orders.

## 13. Practitioner Checklists

### 13.1 Before you start planning

- [ ] What strategic guidance authorizes this? (NSS, NDS, NMS, UCP, CPG, JSCP, GIF, or commander's directive)
- [ ] Who is the supported commander? Who are supporting commanders? Where does coordinating authority rest?
- [ ] Have I distinguished the **symptom** from the **root cause** of this problem?
- [ ] Is military force the right instrument? Could DoS, USAID, Treasury, or law enforcement lead?
- [ ] What is the relationship between the directed military end state and the policymakers' intended *strategic* objective? Are there gaps to surface?

### 13.2 Mission analysis hygiene

- [ ] Mission statement contains **who, what, when, where, why** — and the *purpose* is explicit.
- [ ] Specified, implied, and essential tasks are explicitly separated.
- [ ] Constraints ("must do") and restraints ("cannot do") are distinguished.
- [ ] Friendly and enemy COGs are identified, with critical capabilities/requirements/vulnerabilities for each.
- [ ] CCIRs (PIRs + FFIRs) are short, time-bound, and tied to decision points.
- [ ] Assumptions are minimized; each has a CCIR to validate it.
- [ ] Commander has signed off on planning guidance, intent statement, and refined operational approach.

### 13.3 COA validity (the five-point gate)

- [ ] **Suitable** — accomplishes the mission within commander's guidance?
- [ ] **Feasible** — accomplishable with forces, posture, transportation, logistics, munitions in time/space?
- [ ] **Acceptable** — risk balanced against advantage; reconciled with US/USG law, ROE, policy?
- [ ] **Distinguishable** — main effort, scheme, sequencing, mechanism, task org, or use of reserves materially different?
- [ ] **Complete** — answers who/what/where/when/how/why; objectives, effects, forces, deployment, time, and end-state criteria specified?

### 13.4 Wargaming discipline

- [ ] Wargame against **most likely** AND **most dangerous** enemy COA.
- [ ] Red cell is aggressive and realistic, not a strawman.
- [ ] White cell adjudicates; participants don't argue.
- [ ] Action → Reaction → Counteraction documented in the synchronization matrix.
- [ ] DST, decision points, branches, sequels, refined CCIRs, and shortfalls captured as outputs.
- [ ] If a major revision is needed → **stop, revise, restart** — never patch mid-game.

### 13.5 Final plan check ("Check the Plan" — JP 5-0, IV-45)

- [ ] Plan does **not violate** the principles of joint operations.
- [ ] Joint functions (C2, Information, Intelligence, Fires, Movement & Maneuver, Protection, Sustainment) are addressed, interlaced, and reinforcing.
- [ ] Plan achieves the objective or attains the military end state within acceptable risk.
- [ ] Plan **does not foreclose future options**.

### 13.6 Risk discussion script for civilian leaders
1. State the risk in **mission-success terms**, not military jargon.
2. Quantify where you can ("6 months vs. 2 months", "casualties from x to y").
3. Distinguish **risk-to-mission** from **risk-to-force**.
4. Identify the **opportunity cost** — what other plans/contingencies become harder?
5. Offer mitigation options across all three categories: reduce likelihood, reduce cost, augment with nonorganic support.
6. Name the **residual risk** that cannot be eliminated.
